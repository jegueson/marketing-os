# 1. Executive Summary

## The company thesis

Build a **marketing-only AI operating company** that combines a proprietary marketing cockpit, a deep integration layer and a team of AI marketing specialists who design, deploy and continuously improve tailored AI skills, automations and workflows for each customer.

The customer does not sign up to browse a marketplace of generic agents.

The customer comes to the company with a marketing problem.

A success manager and AI marketing specialist understand the business, connect the customer's existing marketing stack, configure the Marketing OS, build the required skills and automations, and continuously improve the system.

The result is intended to replace a substantial portion of recurring work historically performed by:

- marketing consultants,
- reporting and analytics consultants,
- performance agencies,
- SEO agencies,
- CRM/lifecycle agencies,
- creative production teams,
- marketing operations specialists,
- and fragmented point-solution vendors.

The platform is therefore the **delivery infrastructure for an AI-native marketing service**.

The long-term vision is not simply software licensing. It is to become the **outsourced intelligent operating layer for the marketing department**.

## The core promise

> **Connect your marketing stack. Give us the business problem. We build the AI marketing system that runs it.**

The customer gets:

1. A unified marketing cockpit.
2. A single conversational interface to their connected marketing data.
3. Tailored skills and agents built by AI marketing specialists.
4. Tailored automations and workflows.
5. Integration with the tools they already use.
6. Human governance where decisions or risk require it.
7. Continuous optimization instead of a one-time implementation.

## Strategic shift from the previous plan

The company should **not** be positioned primarily as self-serve SaaS.

The core commercial model is:

**Lead → Discovery → Marketing Operating Audit → Connected Cockpit → Tailored AI System → Managed Execution → Continuous Optimization → Expansion**

The platform remains recurring and scalable, but customer access is provisioned through a commercial relationship rather than open self-service signup.

# 4. The Problem

Modern marketing departments have a stack rather than a system.

A typical customer may have:

- Salesforce or HubSpot,
- GA4,
- Google Ads,
- Meta,
- LinkedIn,
- TikTok,
- Search Console,
- SEO tools,
- CMS,
- experimentation tools,
- customer-data platforms,
- email/marketing automation,
- Asana, Jira or Linear,
- Notion or Confluence,
- creative tools,
- consent-management platforms,
- social listening/review tools,
- spreadsheets,
- data warehouses,
- agency dashboards,
- and internal BI.

The problem is not simply that there is too much data.

The problem is that the data, people, processes and tools are disconnected.

## CMO problem

The CMO asks:

- What is happening?
- Why is it happening?
- What should I do?
- Where should we invest?
- Which agencies or teams are actually adding value?
- What is being missed?
- What should happen this week?

The answers require multiple teams and reports.

## Marketing manager problem

Managers spend large amounts of time:

- collecting data,
- reconciling systems,
- creating recurring reports,
- briefing agencies,
- checking campaigns,
- creating tickets,
- translating insights into tasks,
- QA-ing tracking,
- preparing creative iterations,
- monitoring leads,
- maintaining lifecycle journeys,
- and coordinating specialists.

## Agency/consulting problem

Traditional services are expensive because many tasks require recurring human labor:

- reporting,
- analysis,
- campaign monitoring,
- recommendations,
- content and creative variations,
- optimization,
- research,
- presentations,
- coordination.

AI can automate significant portions of these activities when the AI has sufficient context, data access, skills, permissions and workflow infrastructure.

That is the business opportunity.

# 5. Core Product Concept

The product has two visible layers and one invisible operating layer.

## Customer layer — Marketing Cockpit

A pre-configured dashboard automatically generated from the customer's connected stack.

It is divided into the main marketing areas:

- Executive overview
- Performance marketing
- Organic / SEO / search visibility
- Website / UX / CRO
- Brand & creative
- CRM / retention
- Leads
- Data & analytics
- Market intelligence
- Tracking / consent / governance
- Work and automations

The dashboard should answer:

**What is happening? What matters? What changed? What should happen next?**

## Customer conversation layer — Ask Marketing

A message box allows the user to ask questions about their own connected data.

Examples:

> "Why did qualified leads fall last month?"

> "Which campaigns have the biggest budget waste?"

> "Compare Meta and Google by qualified pipeline, not just conversions."

> "What changed in retention for customers acquired in Q2?"

> "Which creative themes are producing the best conversion rate?"

A customer can configure an LLM/model provider of their choice through an approved connector/contractual setup. The platform should expose a model gateway, but not force one model vendor on the customer.

## Internal operating layer — AI Marketing Specialist Console

This is the hidden force multiplier.

The AI marketing specialist can:

- inspect the customer's stack,
- understand business goals,
- create skills,
- configure agents,
- define prompts,
- load brand guidelines,
- configure knowledge sources,
- create workflows,
- configure automations,
- define policies,
- set permissions,
- connect tools,
- test outputs,
- monitor runs,
- and improve the system.

This means each customer can have a **tailored Marketing OS** without the company having to create a separate software product for every customer.

# 6. User Journey

The definitive customer journey should be:

## Stage 1 — Lead generation

Customer submits a lead form.

No public signup.
No freemium product.
No self-service subscription checkout.

Lead information should capture:

- company,
- role,
- industry,
- markets,
- approximate marketing team size,
- marketing stack,
- business challenge,
- current agencies/consultancies,
- major objectives,
- optional marketing spend range.

## Stage 2 — Success Manager discovery

A success manager contacts the prospect.

The goal is not to sell generic AI.

The objective is to understand:

- business model,
- marketing organization,
- priorities,
- goals,
- problems,
- stack,
- recurring work,
- agency dependency,
- data availability,
- governance requirements,
- and potential automation opportunities.

## Stage 3 — Marketing Operating Audit

Before building significant automation, the specialist maps:

**Goals → Data → Tools → Processes → People → Decisions → Actions → Outcomes**

Output:

- marketing system map,
- integration plan,
- cockpit specification,
- high-value use cases,
- first skill set,
- initial automations,
- business case,
- implementation plan.

## Stage 4 — Provision account

Credentials are sent to the customer.

The customer connects approved systems, for example:

- Salesforce,
- HubSpot,
- Google Analytics,
- Google Ads,
- Meta,
- TikTok,
- LinkedIn,
- Search Console,
- Asana,
- Jira,
- Linear,
- Notion,
- CMS,
- experimentation platforms,
- CDP/data warehouse,
- and other systems.

The customer explicitly selects which accounts, properties and data scopes to connect.

## Stage 5 — Automatic cockpit

Once the first integrations are connected, the platform builds the pre-configured marketing cockpit.

The dashboard structure is standard.
The data, benchmarks, KPIs and views are customer-specific.

## Stage 6 — Ask Marketing

The customer can immediately use the conversational data interface.

This creates early value before deeper automation is deployed.

## Stage 7 — Tailored AI build

The success manager and AI marketing specialist access the customer account.

They configure:

- business context,
- marketing goals,
- brand profile,
- prompts,
- skills,
- agents,
- workflows,
- automations,
- permissions,
- approval rules,
- knowledge sources,
- model preferences.

Examples:

- ComfyUI workflow for branded creative production.
- Automatic weekly performance diagnosis.
- Lead-quality monitoring.
- CRM segment generation.
- SEO content opportunity workflow.
- Creative fatigue detection.
- Automated executive reporting.
- Tracking QA.

## Stage 8 — Managed operation

The company continuously monitors results and improves the customer's Marketing OS.

This creates recurring value and recurring revenue.

# 10. Agency and Consultant Replacement Strategy

Do not market the company as "agents replacing everyone."

The more credible proposition is:

> **Automate and absorb the recurring work currently outsourced to agencies and consultants, while keeping strategic human oversight where it adds value.**

The replacement opportunity is strongest in repeatable services:

- reporting,
- monitoring,
- campaign analysis,
- recurring optimization,
- research,
- content variation,
- creative production,
- CRM operations,
- QA,
- tagging/tracking checks,
- project coordination,
- presentation creation,
- routine recommendation cycles.

Human specialists remain important for:

- complex business strategy,
- brand-defining decisions,
- high-risk decisions,
- governance,
- stakeholder alignment,
- unusual situations,
- and customer relationship management.

Over time, automation should move more recurring work into the product.

The platform should not copy everything blindly.

It should:

2. Understand schemas.

# 32. Major Risks

## Risk 1 — Becoming a traditional agency

Mitigation:

Every repeated service activity must be converted into a skill, workflow, connector or product capability.

## Risk 2 — High implementation cost

Mitigation:

Standardize the onboarding architecture and reuse connection/skill templates.

## Risk 3 — Customers demand unlimited customization

Mitigation:

Three layers:

**Core platform → Configurable modules → Premium custom automation**.

## Risk 4 — Data quality

Mitigation:

Data-health layer and source lineage before autonomous actions.

## Risk 5 — Agent reliability

Mitigation:

Policies, evaluations, approvals, logs and rollback where feasible.

## Risk 6 — Integration maintenance

Mitigation:

Connector SDK and health monitoring.

## Risk 7 — Vendor platform competition

Mitigation:

Remain independent and stack-agnostic.

## Risk 8 — AI commoditization

Mitigation:

Own customer context, workflows, skills, deployment know-how and outcomes rather than the base model.

## Risk 9 — Perceived threat from agencies

Initially position the platform around reducing low-value recurring work rather than indiscriminately eliminating strategic humans.
